## Overview

Paganism is the broad term for every religious tradition that falls outside the Abrahamic faiths. Norse worshippers, Celtic druids, Slavic spirit-callers, African ancestral cults, and countless unnamed village traditions all fall under this umbrella.

While the names of their gods and the details of their rituals differ by region and ethnicity, pagan traditions are remarkably similar in substance. They worship the same kinds of spirits, honor the same forces of nature, and practice the same fundamental rites — only the language and the faces change. A Norse seer reading omens in the entrails of a sacrifice is performing essentially the same act as an African shaman casting bones. The gods they call upon may have different names, but they answer in the same ways.

## Common Characteristics

Despite the enormous diversity, most pagan traditions share certain broad features that distinguish them from the Abrahamic faiths:

**Polytheism or Animism:** Most pagans worship multiple gods, spirits, or ancestors — or believe that the natural world itself is inhabited by divine forces. The idea of a single, jealous God who demands exclusive worship is foreign to most pagan traditions.

**Locality:** Pagan religions are typically bound to a specific people, place, or bloodline. They do not claim universal truth or seek to convert outsiders. A god belongs to his people; he does not demand the worship of strangers.

**Oral Tradition:** Sacred knowledge is passed down through storytelling, song, and ritual rather than through written scripture. This makes pagan traditions resilient to destruction of physical artifacts but vulnerable to the death of their practitioners.

**Ritual Over Doctrine:** Pagan faiths tend to emphasize correct practice — the right offering at the right time, the proper words spoken at the proper place — over correct belief. Orthodoxy matters less than orthopraxy.

**The Sacred in Nature:** Rivers, mountains, forests, and storms are not merely God's creation to be subdued — they are themselves sacred, inhabited by spirits, or manifestations of divine power. This places most pagan traditions in direct philosophical conflict with both Christianity and Islam, which view nature as subordinate to a transcendent Creator.

## Pagan Peoples

Pagan traditions are distinguished primarily by the ethnic group that practices them rather than by any meaningful theological difference. The names and stories change; the practices do not.

- **Norse Pagans** — Scandinavia and its settlements. Their gods are the Aesir and Vanir — Odin, Thor, Freya, Tyr. They emphasize honor, fate, and fearlessness in battle.
- **Celtic Pagans** — Britain, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, and parts of Gaul. Their priests are druids, their holy sites are sacred groves and stone circles.
- **Slavic Pagans** — Eastern Europe. Their gods bear names like Perun, Veles, and Mokosh. Ancestor worship is especially prominent.
- **African Pagans** — Encountered primarily through the crusades and trade. Their traditions center on ancestor spirits and animistic practices.
- **Eastern Pagans** — Hindu, Buddhist, and other traditions of the far east. The least understood by European Christians, often dismissed as demon worship.

In practice, a party encountering pagans in any region can expect the same general behaviors — spirit worship, nature reverence, ritual sacrifice, oral tradition, and a deep suspicion of anyone carrying a cross or a crescent.

## Pagans and the Abrahamic Faiths

### The Christian View

The Church regards pagans as souls in darkness — worshipping demons, dead men, or empty idols in place of the true God. Evangelization is the preferred response, but where pagans resist conversion or threaten Christian communities, suppression is considered justified.

Pagans are considered more salvageable than heretics — they sin from ignorance rather than from willful rejection of known truth. A pagan who converts is welcomed. A pagan who refuses after hearing the Gospel is an obstacle.

### The Muslim View

Islam offers pagans no protected status. Where Christians and Jews may live as dhimmis under Muslim rule, pagans are given a starker choice — convert or face the sword. The Quran commands Muslims to fight polytheists until they submit to God alone.

### Among Themselves

Most pagan traditions do not view other religions as threats to be eliminated. A Norse warrior might acknowledge that the Christian God exists, he simply does not worship Him. This theological flexibility makes pagans poor crusaders but relatively tolerant neighbors, at least until someone tries to cut down their sacred oak.

## In the World

Paganism is in retreat across most of the known world. Christianity dominates Europe. Islam holds the east and south. The old ways survive in the margins — the deep forests, the remote islands, the mountain villages where no priest has ever set foot and no imam has ever called the faithful to prayer.

Yet paganism is far from dead. In Scandinavia, the Norse gods are still invoked openly. In the British Isles, druidic knowledge passes quietly from master to student. In the Slavic east, the old festivals continue under thin Christian disguise. And in Africa and the far east, traditions older than either Christianity or Islam continue unbroken, largely indifferent to the theological arguments of distant empires.

For the crusading orders operating in occupied territories, pagans represent a secondary concern — nuisances rather than existential threats. But in Europe proper, the persistence of pagan practice is a source of ongoing irritation for the Church, a reminder that the Christianization of the continent is still, after centuries, incomplete.
